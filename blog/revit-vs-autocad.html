Revit vs AutoCAD: Which Should Engineers Learn First?

📅 April 2, 2026 ✍ Awet G. Nway 🕑 5 min read

This is one of the most common questions from engineering students and early-career professionals: Should I learn AutoCAD or Revit first? The internet is full of conflicting advice. This article gives you the honest, experience-based answer — with no agenda except helping you make the right decision for your career.

The short answer: AutoCAD first, then Revit. But the real answer depends on your specialization. Let me explain why.

What AutoCAD Actually Is

AutoCAD is a 2D drafting and 3D modelling tool developed by Autodesk, first released in 1982. It is the closest software equivalent of a traditional drawing board — you draw lines, arcs, and shapes in precise geometry. What you draw is exactly what appears in the final document. AutoCAD is geometry-based: a line is just a line, a rectangle is just a rectangle, a circle is just a circle. There is no intelligence built into the objects.

AutoCAD's strength is its universality. It is used in every engineering discipline — electrical, mechanical, civil, structural, plant design, and architecture. DWG files (AutoCAD's native format) are an industry-wide standard accepted by virtually every other CAD software. Learning AutoCAD gives you a skill that transfers across industries and across the world.

What Revit Actually Is

Revit is a Building Information Modelling (BIM) platform. Unlike AutoCAD, Revit is not a drawing tool — it is a data-rich 3D model that automatically generates drawings. When you place a wall in Revit, it is not a collection of lines — it is an intelligent object that knows its material, thickness, fire rating, thermal properties, and cost. Change the wall height in one view, and it updates everywhere automatically.

Revit is designed for multidisciplinary building projects: architecture, structural engineering, mechanical and electrical systems (MEP), and facilities management — all in one coordinated model. It is the dominant platform in large building projects, infrastructure, and government work in most developed countries.

■ AutoCAD — Strengths

  • Universal — used in every engineering field
  • DWG format accepted everywhere
  • Fast to learn, fast to produce simple drawings
  • Cheaper (AutoCAD LT available)
  • Works for 2D deliverables (as-built drawings, schematics)
  • Essential for plant & piping (AutoCAD P&ID)
  • Strong job market in developing countries

■ Revit — Strengths

  • Full 3D building model, not just drawings
  • Clash detection across disciplines
  • Automatic drawing generation from 3D model
  • Quantity takeoff and cost estimation built in
  • Industry standard for large building projects
  • Required on government/international projects
  • Higher salary potential for BIM specialists

The Key Difference That Engineers Miss

Most people frame this as "AutoCAD vs Revit" as if they are alternatives. They are not. AutoCAD teaches you how to draw. Revit teaches you how to model. These are fundamentally different skills, and both matter.

An engineer who learns Revit without AutoCAD foundations often struggles with basic drafting discipline — drawing standards, line weights, scale, dimensioning, annotation. These fundamentals come naturally from AutoCAD and make your Revit output far more professional. Revit handles the 3D model; you still need drafting sense to produce clear, readable construction documents from it.

Which to Learn First — By Specialization

Structural Engineers

Learn AutoCAD first for reinforcement details, connection drawings, and as-built documentation. Add Revit Structure for large building projects. Structural engineers also commonly use SAP2000, ETABS, or STAAD for analysis — none of which are replaced by Revit.

Civil Engineers

AutoCAD Civil 3D is the dominant tool for roads, grading, drainage, and land development. Revit is rarely used in pure civil work. Civil engineers should focus on AutoCAD Civil 3D and add GIS skills (ArcGIS or QGIS) before considering Revit.

MEP Engineers (Mechanical, Electrical, Plumbing)

This is where Revit MEP is most valuable. Large building projects increasingly require coordinated MEP models for clash detection. However, AutoCAD MEP remains common for smaller projects and as-built documentation. Learn AutoCAD first, then Revit MEP.

Architects

Revit Architecture is the professional standard for architectural practice. Start with Revit — but still learn basic AutoCAD, because client-supplied drawings and site survey data often arrive as DWG files.

Industrial / Plant Engineers

AutoCAD is dominant — particularly AutoCAD Plant 3D and AutoCAD P&ID for piping schematics. Revit has limited application in industrial facilities. Stick with AutoCAD and learn SmartPlant or AVEVA for process plant work.

The Practical Learning Path

  1. Month 1–2: AutoCAD fundamentals — Drawing setup, coordinate systems, precision drawing, layers, blocks, dimensioning, and plotting. Complete 5–10 real drawing exercises (not tutorials).
  2. Month 3: AutoCAD for your specialization — Civil 3D, Electrical, Mechanical, or Architecture, depending on your field.
  3. Month 4–6: Revit fundamentals — Project setup, levels and grids, walls, floors, roofs (Architecture) or beams, columns, foundations (Structure) or ducts, pipes, conduit (MEP).
  4. Month 7+: Advanced Revit — Families, schedules, sheets, coordination, IFC export, BIM 360 collaboration.
"AutoCAD is the language of technical drawing. Revit is the language of building information. A professional engineer needs both — but you cannot run before you walk."

🎯 Final Verdict

Learn AutoCAD first (1–3 months). It builds the drafting discipline that every engineer needs. Then learn Revit for your specialization (3–6 months). If you work on building projects, Revit will eventually become your primary tool. If you work in industrial, civil, or plant engineering, AutoCAD will remain dominant throughout your career. Either way, having both makes you significantly more employable.
