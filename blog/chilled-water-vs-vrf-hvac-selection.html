Chilled Water vs VRF: How to Choose the Right HVAC System for Any Building

📅 June 5, 2026 ✍ Awet G. Nway 🕑 11 min read
🌐 Read in:

The choice between a chilled water system and a VRF (Variable Refrigerant Flow) system is the most consequential HVAC design decision on most commercial projects. Get it right and you have a system that meets every comfort requirement, costs the right amount to run, and the client will thank you for over the next 25 years. Get it wrong and you will spend years managing noise complaints, energy bills nobody budgeted for, and refrigerant leaks in occupied spaces.

The challenge is that both systems have genuine strengths, and neither is universally superior. The decision depends on building size, usage patterns, climate, available plant space, client budget (capital vs operational), and the local supply chain for spare parts and maintenance. This article gives you a structured framework for making that decision correctly, with real performance data and the factors most engineers miss.

"Both chilled water and VRF are excellent HVAC technologies. The question is never which is better — it is which is better for this building, this client, this location, and this budget."

1. How Each System Works — The Fundamentals

💧 Chilled Water (CHW) System

A central chiller (water-cooled or air-cooled) produces chilled water at 6–12°C. This water is pumped through insulated pipes to air handling units (AHUs) and fan coil units (FCUs) throughout the building. The water absorbs heat from room air and returns to the chiller at 12–16°C. The refrigerant (in the chiller only) then rejects this heat to the condenser — either to the atmosphere via a cooling tower or to outdoor air via an air-cooled condenser.

  • ✓ Best energy efficiency at large scale (COP 5.0–7.0+)
  • ✓ Refrigerant contained in plant room only
  • ✓ Unlimited pipe run distances
  • ✗ Highest first cost at small scale
  • ✗ Large plant room required
  • ✗ Cooling tower = Legionella risk management

⚡ VRF/VRV System

Variable Refrigerant Flow (VRF, also called VRV — Daikin's tradename) uses refrigerant directly as the heat transfer medium throughout the building. An outdoor unit (condenser) connects via refrigerant pipes to multiple indoor units (fan coils) in each room. The outdoor unit modulates compressor speed to exactly match the instantaneous cooling demand of all connected indoor units simultaneously.

  • ✓ Low first cost at small-medium scale
  • ✓ Individual zone control (each room independently)
  • ✓ Heat recovery possible (heat pump mode)
  • ✗ Refrigerant in occupied spaces (leak risk)
  • ✗ Pipe run limitations (typically 150m max)
  • ✗ Higher maintenance complexity

2. The Definitive Side-by-Side Comparison

Parameter 💧 Chilled Water ⚡ VRF System
Refrigerant location Plant room only. No refrigerant in occupied spaces or distribution pipework. Refrigerant throughout the building in every zone. F-Gas regulations apply to all connected piping.
Peak efficiency (COP) COP 4.5–7.0 at full load (water-cooled centrifugal). Best at large scale. COP 3.5–5.5 at part load. Inverter compressor optimises at partial load — most efficient between 40–80% load.
Part-load performance Variable speed chillers achieve good part-load efficiency. Multiple chillers staged for better part-load coverage. Excellent part-load performance — inverter-driven compressor matches demand precisely. Outperforms CHW in mixed-use variable occupancy buildings.
Heating capability Separate hot water boiler system required. LTHW distribution added alongside CHW pipework. Heat pump VRF provides both cooling and heating from same system. Heat recovery VRF (3-pipe) can cool some zones while heating others simultaneously.
First cost (capital) Higher for small buildings (<500 kW). Becomes cost-competitive above 1,000 kW where economies of scale apply. Lower first cost for small-medium buildings (50–800 kW). Competitive installation speed. No plant room required (or much smaller).
Operational cost Lower for large buildings with high simultaneous loads. Water-cooled chillers very efficient. Cooling tower water costs. Lower for part-load operation (offices, hotels with variable occupancy). Heat pump eliminates boiler fuel cost.
Pipe run limitations None practical — chilled water can be pumped hundreds of metres with booster pumps. Standard for high-rise buildings. Maximum 150–175m equivalent pipe length (manufacturer-dependent). Height difference limit of 50–90m (outdoor above) or 40m (outdoor below). Critical constraint for tall buildings.
Zone control Zone control via 2-port or 3-port motorised valves and zone temperature sensors. FCUs have individual fan speed control. Superior individual zone control — each indoor unit independently controlled. Better suited to buildings where different zones have very different schedules.
Maintenance Chiller maintenance annual. Cooling tower cleaning (Legionella). All in plant room — does not require access to occupied zones. Refrigerant leak checks required (F-Gas). Each indoor unit needs periodic filter cleaning. Manufacturer-specific training often required for VRF maintenance.
Spare parts availability Standard components widely available (pumps, valves, coils). Not brand-specific for most distribution components. Highly brand-specific. VRF spare parts often only available from the original manufacturer. Long lead times in some regions.
Noise Chiller noise in plant room only. FCUs very quiet (25–45 dB). Cooling tower noise can be significant — requires acoustic treatment on rooftop. Outdoor unit noise can be significant if near offices/residences. Indoor unit noise 26–38 dB. VRF outdoor units near car parks or roof edges can cause complaints.
Reliability / redundancy N+1 chiller redundancy easily designed. Failure of one chiller does not affect entire system if capacity allows. Failure of outdoor unit takes down all connected indoor units. N+1 outdoor unit design is possible but expensive. No cross-building backup inherent in design.
F-Gas / refrigerant regulations Chiller contains refrigerant. HFO refrigerants (R1234ze, R1234yf) low GWP options available. Contained to plant room. Large refrigerant charge throughout building. GWP of R410A (common VRF refrigerant) = 2,088. Moving to R32 (GWP 675) or HFOs. Increasing regulatory scrutiny in EU and UK.

3. The Decision Framework — Building Type by Type

🏢

Residential Apartments (1–50 units) VRF Wins

VRF's individual zone control perfectly suits residential buildings where each apartment has completely different occupancy and comfort preferences. Small plant footprint, no cooling tower, heat pump capability for heating. Capital cost is significantly lower than chilled water at this scale. Refrigerant-in-occupied-space concern is manageable for small residential charges.

🏢

Hotels (50–300 rooms) VRF Wins

Hotels have the ideal VRF operating profile: variable occupancy (80–100% on weekends, 40% on weekdays), independent zone control for guest comfort, heat pump heating. VRF's excellent part-load efficiency directly matches hotel load patterns. Individual room control is commercially important — guests can set their own temperature. Consider 3-pipe heat recovery VRF in climates requiring simultaneous heating and cooling.

🏢

Office Buildings (2,000–20,000 m²) Depends on Size

Small offices (<2,000 m²): VRF wins on first cost and simplicity. Medium offices (2,000–10,000 m²): genuine competition — model both life-cycle costs. Large offices (>10,000 m²): chilled water wins on lifecycle cost, energy efficiency, and central monitoring capability. High-rise offices (>10 storeys): chilled water wins outright — VRF pipe run limitations make it impractical or require complex multiple outdoor unit arrangements.

🏦

Hospitals and Healthcare CHW Wins

Hospitals require: (a) refrigerant-free occupied zones (refrigerant leaks in an ICU or operating theatre are catastrophic), (b) close humidity control (chilled water with AHU dehumidification is far more capable), (c) 100% outside air in surgical areas (VRF cannot handle 100% OA economically), (d) multiple levels of redundancy. Chilled water is the only technically appropriate choice for hospitals.

🏫

Schools and Universities Mixed Approach

Classrooms and offices: VRF works well — good part-load efficiency for variable occupancy, good individual control. Laboratories, sports halls, and auditoriums: chilled water AHU units better handle the high ventilation rates and large zones. Many universities use both: chilled water for central plant and large spaces, VRF for office wings and residential buildings.

🏢

Data Centres CHW Wins

Data centres have continuous, high-density, predictable cooling loads — the opposite of VRF's sweet spot. Chilled water with precision air cooling (CRAC/CRAH) units is the standard solution. Water-cooled chillers provide the highest efficiency for 24/7 base loads. VRF is never specified for primary data centre cooling.

🏢

High-Rise Buildings (>10 floors) CHW Wins

VRF pipe run limits (typically 150m equivalent length, 90m height) make multi-zone coverage of tall buildings either impossible or require separate outdoor units per 5–6 floors — negating the cost advantage. Chilled water systems with booster pumping zones have no such height limitations. Central chiller plants with distributed FCUs are standard for any building above 10 storeys.

🏜

Industrial Facilities / Warehouses CHW Wins

Large open-plan spaces with high heat loads from equipment, people, and process heat are best served by central plant and AHUs with large duct runs. VRF indoor units are not suited to large open spaces — you would need many units creating uneven air distribution. Industrial facilities may also have special requirements (explosion-proof areas, high dust) incompatible with standard VRF indoor units.

4. Life-Cycle Cost Analysis — The Number Everybody Forgets

First cost is what most clients focus on during design. Life-cycle cost (capital + energy + maintenance + replacement over 25 years) is what actually matters. For HVAC systems, energy cost over 25 years typically exceeds the capital cost 3–5 times. A system that costs $50,000 more to install but saves $5,000/year in energy is 25-year NPV positive within 10 years.

📉 Simple Life-Cycle Cost Calculation

Annual energy cost = Peak load (kW) × Annual full-load equivalent hours × Electricity rate ($/kWh) ÷ System COP

Example: 500 kW cooling load, 2,500 full-load hours/year, $0.15/kWh:
• CHW system (COP 5.5): 500 × 2,500 × 0.15 ÷ 5.5 = $34,090/year
• VRF system (COP 4.0): 500 × 2,500 × 0.15 ÷ 4.0 = $46,875/year
• Annual saving with CHW: $12,785/year
• Over 25 years (simple): $319,625 energy savings

If CHW costs $200,000 more to install, it pays back in 15.6 years on energy alone — and 25-year NPV favours CHW by over $100,000 at 5% discount rate.

5. The Factors Most Engineers Miss

F-Gas Regulations Are Tightening

The EU F-Gas Regulation (2024 revision) and UK F-Gas phase-down schedule are progressively reducing the availability and increasing the cost of high-GWP refrigerants. VRF systems using R410A (GWP 2,088) will face increasing maintenance costs as R410A prices rise due to phase-down. Systems using R32 (GWP 675) are better positioned. CHW systems, with refrigerant contained to the plant room, are better isolated from end-user F-Gas compliance requirements. This regulatory risk should be factored into 25-year lifecycle cost projections.

Spare Parts Availability in Your Region

VRF systems are highly manufacturer-specific. In many markets in East Africa and the Middle East, VRF spare parts availability is limited — long lead times for outdoor unit compressors, PCB boards, and expansion valves are common. A failed outdoor unit in a hotel during peak season that takes 6–8 weeks to repair because the part must be shipped from Japan is a real and expensive risk. Chilled water systems use standard industrial components (pumps, motors, valves) available locally from multiple suppliers.

The Refrigerant-in-Occupied-Space Risk

VRF indoor units contain refrigerant pipe connections inside rooms. A refrigerant leak in an occupied space creates several risks: oxygen displacement in enclosed areas, skin and eye irritation, and environmental discharge. EN 378 / ISO 5149 set maximum refrigerant concentrations in occupied spaces — for large VRF systems, the charge calculation becomes a critical design constraint. In hospitals, schools, and childcare facilities, this risk drives the specification toward chilled water regardless of other factors.

BMS Integration and Central Monitoring

Chilled water systems integrate naturally with Building Management Systems (BMS) using standard protocols (BACnet, Modbus). Central energy metering, fault detection, and trending are straightforward. VRF systems historically used proprietary communication protocols — though modern systems now offer BACnet gateways, the integration is more complex and expensive. For buildings where central energy management is a priority (large offices, campuses, hospitals), chilled water offers better BMS integration.

⚠ The Hybrid Approach — Often the Best Answer

Many large projects use a hybrid approach: chilled water for the main building (large floors, AHUs, central zones) and VRF for ancillary buildings, server rooms, or areas with highly variable occupancy. A hotel might use CHW for the central public areas, restaurants, and function rooms, and VRF for the guest room wings. This hybrid approach captures the strengths of both systems — but requires careful design coordination to avoid complexity that increases maintenance cost.

6. Quick Selection Checklist — Decide in 5 Minutes

Answer these questions. If most answers point the same direction, that is your system.

  • ☑ Building height >10 floors? → Chilled water (VRF pipe limits)
  • ☑ Hospital, operating theatre, or cleanroom? → Chilled water (no refrigerant in zone)
  • ☑ 100% outside air required for any zone? → Chilled water AHU
  • ☑ Cooling load >1,500 kW total? → Chilled water (economies of scale)
  • ☑ Need simultaneous heating and cooling in different zones? → VRF heat recovery (3-pipe)
  • ☑ Variable occupancy (hotel, serviced apartments)? → VRF (part-load efficiency)
  • ☑ No plant room space available? → VRF (outdoor units only)
  • ☑ VRF spare parts not readily available locally? → Chilled water (standard components)
  • ☑ Client prioritises lowest first cost? → VRF (for <1,000 kW)
  • ☑ Client prioritises lowest 25-year lifecycle cost? → Chilled water (for >500 kW continuous load)
  • ☑ Retrofitting into existing building with no duct space? → VRF (small-diameter refrigerant pipes vs large ductwork)
  • ☑ Central energy monitoring and BMS integration priority? → Chilled water
