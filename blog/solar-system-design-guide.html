How to Design an Off-Grid Solar System:
The Engineer's Complete Guide

📅 June 3, 2026 ✍ Awet G. Nway 🕑 14 min read
🌐 Read in:

Designing a solar system is not guesswork. It is a structured engineering process with a defined sequence of calculations, each one feeding into the next. When done correctly, the system produces reliable power for 20–25 years with minimal maintenance. When done incorrectly — using rules of thumb, oversimplified online calculators, or vendor-driven "recommendations" — the result is a system that either fails within five years or never delivers what the client was promised.

This article walks through the complete off-grid solar design process in eight steps, using a real design example throughout — a small school in rural Tigray, Ethiopia that needs reliable electricity for lighting, a computer lab, water pumping, and administration. By the end, you will be able to design a correctly-sized, properly protected, professionally specified off-grid solar system for any similar application.

Step 1 — Define the Load Schedule

1

Build a Complete Electrical Load Schedule

This is the foundation of the entire design. Every appliance must be listed with its power rating in watts, quantity, and daily hours of operation. Do not estimate — verify every load with the appliance nameplate or manufacturer datasheet. Guessing loads leads to undersized systems that fail to meet demand.

For our school in Tigray, the load schedule is:

✎ The 20% Safety Margin Rule

Always add a 20% safety margin to your total daily load to account for inverter losses (typically 5–8%), wiring losses (1–3%), and loads you may have forgotten or that may be added in future. Design load = 14,250 Wh × 1.20 = 17,100 Wh/day. This is the figure we use for all subsequent calculations.

Step 2 — Determine the Solar Resource

2

Find Peak Sun Hours (PSH) for Your Location

Peak Sun Hours is the number of hours per day when solar irradiance equals 1,000 W/m² — the standard test condition for panel ratings. A panel rated 400 Wp will produce 400 Wh per Peak Sun Hour. In Tigray, the average PSH is 6.0 hours/day annually, though this varies from 5.2 (rainy season) to 6.8 (dry season). For system design, always use the worst-month PSH to ensure the system meets demand in the most challenging conditions.

Use the Global Solar Atlas (globalsolaratlas.info) or NASA POWER database for your exact coordinates. For a highland school near Adwa, Tigray:

  • Annual average PSH: 6.0 hours/day
  • Worst month (July — rainy season): 4.8 hours/day
  • Best month (November — dry season): 7.1 hours/day
  • Design PSH (use worst month): 4.8 hours/day
⚠ Always Design for the Worst Month

A common mistake is using the annual average PSH. If you design for 6.0 hours and the worst month delivers 4.8 hours, your system will fail to meet demand during the rainy season — exactly when schools and clinics need reliable power most. Design for 4.8 hours; the system will over-produce in the dry season, which simply results in excess charge going to the batteries.

Step 3 — Size the Solar Panel Array

3

Calculate Required Panel kWp

The panel array must produce enough energy on the worst day to meet the design load, accounting for system losses. The derating factor accounts for temperature losses (panels lose ~0.4%/°C above 25°C), dust accumulation, wiring losses, and MPPT efficiency.

Panel Array Sizing Calculation
Design load: 17,100 Wh/day Peak Sun Hours (worst month): 4.8 hours/day System derating factor: 0.78 (typical for Tigray — heat + dust) Required kWp = Design load ÷ (PSH × Derating) Required kWp = 17,100 ÷ (4.8 × 1,000 × 0.78) Required kWp = 17,100 ÷ 3,744 Required kWp = 4.57 kWp Using 400 Wp panels: 4,570 ÷ 400 = 11.4 panels → Install 12 × 400 Wp panels = 4.8 kWp array Always round UP to the next whole number of panels

Temperature Derating — Critical for Hot Climates

Panel output at standard test condition (STC) is rated at 25°C cell temperature. In Tigray's lowlands, cell temperature can reach 60–65°C on hot sunny days. For every 1°C above 25°C, monocrystalline panels lose approximately 0.35–0.40% of rated power. At 60°C cell temperature, that is a 12.25–14% power loss.

Temperature Derating Calculation
Panel temperature coefficient: -0.38%/°C (typical monocrystalline) Maximum cell temperature: 62°C (ambient 40°C + NOCT correction) Temperature rise above STC: 62 - 25 = 37°C Temperature derating: 1 - (0.38% × 37) = 1 - 14.06% = 85.9% of STC power At peak (14:00, summer): one 400 Wp panel actually delivers: 400 × 0.859 = 343.6 Wp effective output This is why 12 panels instead of 12 is better — temperature losses are real and significant

Step 4 — Size the Battery Bank

4

Calculate Battery Capacity Required

The battery bank must store enough energy to power the school for the required autonomy period (2 days) without any solar input — during extended cloudy periods or rainy season. Battery sizing depends on the chemistry: LiFePO4 allows 80% Depth of Discharge (DoD); lead-acid allows only 50% DoD to preserve battery life.

Battery Bank Sizing — LiFePO4
Design load: 17,100 Wh/day Autonomy required: 2 days Battery DoD (LiFePO4): 80% maximum Battery round-trip efficiency: 95% Energy needed in battery bank: = 17,100 × 2 days ÷ 0.80 DoD ÷ 0.95 efficiency = 34,200 ÷ 0.76 = 45,000 Wh = 45 kWh battery bank capacity At 48V system voltage: Capacity in Ah = 45,000 Wh ÷ 48V = 937.5 Ah Using 200 Ah / 48V LiFePO4 batteries: Batteries required = 937.5 ÷ 200 = 4.7 → 5 × 200 Ah batteries in parallel All batteries connected in parallel at 48V system voltage

Why LiFePO4 Instead of Lead-Acid in 2026?

The price of lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries has fallen over 85% since 2018. In 2026, the total cost of ownership (TCO) of LiFePO4 is now lower than lead-acid when you account for the full 10-year lifecycle:

  • LiFePO4 cycle life: 3,000–6,000 cycles at 80% DoD — approximately 8–16 years daily cycling
  • Lead-acid cycle life: 500–800 cycles at 50% DoD — approximately 1.5–2.5 years daily cycling
  • Over 10 years: You buy 1 set of LiFePO4 vs 4–6 sets of lead-acid. At equal replacement cost, lead-acid costs 4–6× more over the system lifetime.
  • Efficiency: LiFePO4: 95–98% round-trip. Lead-acid: 70–85%. More solar energy reaches your loads.
  • Maintenance: LiFePO4 requires zero maintenance. Flooded lead-acid requires monthly water top-up and annual terminal cleaning.

Step 5 — Select the MPPT Charge Controller

5

Size the MPPT Charge Controller

The MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) charge controller converts variable panel voltage to the battery charging voltage, tracking the optimal operating point. It must handle the full array output and must not exceed its maximum input voltage (Voc) — exceeding this instantly destroys the MPPT permanently.

MPPT Charge Controller Sizing
System voltage: 48V Total array power: 4,800 Wp (12 × 400 Wp) Panel Voc (open circuit): 48V per panel (typical 400 Wp mono) Panel Vmp (operating): 40V per panel MPPT output current (battery charging): I_MPPT = Array Wp ÷ Battery voltage × 1.25 safety I_MPPT = 4,800 ÷ 48 × 1.25 = 125A MPPT output required Panel string configuration for 48V system: Vmp target: 48V × 1.25 (MPPT operating range) = 60V minimum input Panels in series: 2 panels × 40V Vmp = 80V Vmp per string ✓ Number of strings: 12 panels ÷ 2 = 6 strings of 2 panels in series Total array Voc: 2 × 48V = 96V — well within typical MPPT max input of 150V → Select: 150A MPPT controller, 48V, max input 150V (use two 75A units in parallel)
⚠ The Voc Temperature Rule — Never Skip This

Panel Voc increases as temperature decreases. In Tigray's highlands, winter night temperatures reach 5°C. At 5°C, panel Voc can be 10–15% higher than the STC datasheet value. If 2 panels in series give Voc = 96V at 25°C, at 5°C they may reach 104–108V. Always verify the cold-temperature Voc stays below the MPPT maximum input voltage. Formula: Voc_cold = Voc_STC × [1 + (Temp_coeff × (T_min − 25))]. For a panel with temp coefficient −0.3%/°C at 5°C: Voc_cold = 48 × [1 + (−0.003 × −20)] = 48 × 1.06 = 50.9V per panel → 2 in series = 101.8V. Still within 150V max — safe.

Step 6 — Select the Inverter

6

Size the Off-Grid Inverter / Charger

The inverter converts DC battery power to 230V AC for your loads. For off-grid systems, always use a hybrid inverter-charger that combines solar MPPT, battery management, and AC inverter in one unit — Victron, Growatt, Solax, and Deye are the leading brands in 2026. The inverter must handle the peak simultaneous load, not just the average.

Inverter Sizing — Peak Load Calculation
Simultaneous peak loads (worst case — school assembly): Classroom lights: 15 × 20W = 300W Computer lab: 10 × 120W = 1,200W Water pump (surge): 750W × 3 (motor starting surge) = 2,250W Office equipment: 200W Security lights: 4 × 60W = 240W Peak simultaneous demand: 300 + 1,200 + 2,250 + 200 + 240 = 4,190W Add 25% safety margin: 4,190 × 1.25 = 5,237W → Select: 6 kW hybrid inverter-charger, 48V DC input, 230V AC output, pure sine wave The pump's starting surge (3× rated power) is the largest single factor — always calculate motor starting current

Pure Sine Wave — Non-Negotiable for This Application

Never use a modified sine wave inverter for computers, medical equipment, or motors. Modified sine wave inverters produce a stepped approximation of AC that damages sensitive electronics, reduces motor efficiency by 15–20%, and can cause overheating in transformers. For a school computer lab, only a pure sine wave inverter is acceptable. The cost difference is typically 20–30% — well worth it for the equipment protection.

Step 7 — Size DC Cables and Protection

7

Cable Sizing and Overcurrent Protection

Undersized DC cables are the number one cause of solar system fires. DC arcs do not self-extinguish like AC arcs — a loose connection or undersized fuse at 48V can sustain an arc indefinitely and start a fire. All cables must be sized for both current-carrying capacity (ampacity) and voltage drop (maximum 1–3% on DC side).

DC Cable Sizing — Battery to Inverter (Critical Circuit)
Inverter rated current: 6,000W ÷ 48V = 125A DC Design current (×1.25): 125 × 1.25 = 156.25A From IEC ampacity tables — cable in free air, 40°C ambient: 50mm² cable rated: 145A → too small 70mm² cable rated: 185A → adequate ✓ Voltage drop check (cable length: 1.5m each way = 3m total): ΔV = (2 × L × I × ρ) ÷ A ΔV = (2 × 1.5 × 156 × 0.0172) ÷ 70 ΔV = 0.115V (0.24% of 48V) → PASS → Use 70mm² DC solar cable, ANL fuse 200A within 30cm of battery positive terminal Always use solar-rated DC cable (EN 50618) — standard AC cable degrades under UV and heat

Protection Devices Required

  • Battery to inverter: 200A ANL fuse within 30cm of battery positive terminal — protects against catastrophic short circuit
  • Panel strings to combiner box: 15A DC fuse per string — protects against panel-to-panel backfeed
  • Array to MPPT: DC isolator switch (lockable) on positive conductor — for safe maintenance isolation
  • MPPT to battery: DC circuit breaker rated for charging current + 25%
  • Inverter AC output to distribution board: MCB sized to inverter output current, plus 30mA RCD
  • Surge protection: Type 2 DC SPD at MPPT input terminals — protects against lightning transients (critical in highland Ethiopia where storms are frequent)

Step 8 — Earthing, Grounding, and Final System Check

8

System Earthing and Final Commissioning Check

A solar system without proper earthing is dangerous and non-compliant. All metal chassis (panel frames, inverter, MPPT, battery enclosure) must be bonded to a single earth bus bar connected to a copper earth rod driven 2.4m into the ground. This provides protection against insulation failure, lightning transients, and eliminates dangerous touch voltages on equipment chassis.

✓ Final System Specification Summary
  • Solar array: 12 × 400 Wp monocrystalline panels = 4.8 kWp | Configuration: 6 strings of 2S in parallel
  • Battery bank: 5 × 200 Ah LiFePO4 at 48V = 48 kWh usable capacity (at 80% DoD)
  • MPPT controller: 2 × 75A MPPT units in parallel | 48V system | max input 150V
  • Inverter-charger: 6 kW hybrid pure sine wave | 48V DC | 230V AC
  • Battery to inverter cable: 70mm² EN 50618 solar DC cable | 200A ANL fuse
  • Panel string cables: 6mm² EN 50618 | 15A DC fuse per string
  • Protection: DC isolator × 2 | SPD Type 2 DC and AC | 30mA RCD on AC output
  • Earthing: All chassis to earth bus bar | 2.4m copper earth rod | 16mm² earth cable
  • Expected daily generation (worst month): 4.8 kWp × 4.8 PSH × 0.78 = 17.9 kWh → meets 17.1 kWh design load ✓

Common Design Mistakes — And How to Avoid Them

  • Using annual average PSH instead of worst-month PSH. Designs the system for good weather — fails in rainy season when reliability matters most. Always design for worst month.
  • Ignoring temperature derating. A panel rated 400 Wp at 25°C produces 340 Wp at 60°C. At array level, this means a 4.8 kWp array effectively delivers 4.1 kWp at peak heat — size accordingly.
  • No safety margin on the load estimate. Loads always grow. A school that needs a new classroom or computer next year suddenly overloads a system designed with zero margin. Always add 20% minimum.
  • Incorrect string voltage calculation. The most common MPPT-destroying mistake — connecting too many panels in series exceeds the maximum input voltage. Always calculate cold-temperature Voc before finalising string configuration.
  • Undersized battery-to-inverter cables. The most common fire risk. A 6 kW inverter draws 125A from a 48V battery. 16mm² cable — which some installers use — is rated for 73A. It will overheat and potentially cause a fire under full load. Size cables properly.
  • No surge protection device (SPD). Lightning is frequent in highland Ethiopia. An unprotected MPPT or inverter can be destroyed by a nearby lightning strike — losing thousands of dollars of equipment in a microsecond. A Type 2 SPD costs $30–80 and provides essential protection.
  • Mixing old and new batteries. Adding a new battery to an existing string of aged batteries causes the new battery to charge the old ones rather than powering loads. Always replace the entire battery bank simultaneously.

Maintenance Schedule — Keeping the System Running for 20 Years

  • Monthly: Visual inspection — check for loose connections, corroded terminals, physical damage to panels or cables. Clean panel surfaces if dust-covered (dry season).
  • Quarterly: Check battery voltage and state of charge logs on the MPPT/BMS. Clean panel surfaces thoroughly. Check that all DC and AC breakers operate correctly. Inspect earth rod connection.
  • Annually: Thermal imaging of all connections (hired specialist). Clean and torque all DC connections. Test earth rod resistance (should be below 10 ohms). Update firmware on inverter and MPPT controllers. Review system performance data — compare actual daily generation vs design values. Any unexplained shortfall indicates a panel failure or degraded component.
  • Every 5 years: Full system audit. Replace any degraded fuses or circuit breakers. Check DC cable insulation resistance. Panels degrade approximately 0.5–0.8% per year — after 10 years, array output is approximately 92–95% of original.
